How Many Books Should You Read in 2027? A Gentle Reading Goal Plan (and Three Ways to Track It)

This guide features products from our own shop, Lantern & Loam. Our designs are created with the help of AI design tools and printed to order by our production partner, Printify.

Every January the same number shows up in our heads: 52 books, one a week. Then February arrives, the stack hasn't moved, and the goal quietly becomes a source of guilt. This guide takes the opposite approach. Choose a number that fits your real life, pick a tracking method you will actually touch, and plan a kind check-in for when things slip.

Start with minutes, not books

A book count means very little until you turn it into time. As a rough estimate, an average 300-page book takes around six hours to read at a comfortable pace. Your speed will differ, so treat these as our own back-of-the-envelope numbers and adjust.

  • 6 books a year: about 36 hours total, or roughly 6 minutes a day.
  • 12 books a year (one a month): about 72 hours, or roughly 12 minutes a day.
  • 24 books a year: about 144 hours, or roughly 24 minutes a day.
  • 52 books a year: about 312 hours, or roughly 50 minutes a day.

Now look at your real week. If you commute by train, have a long lunch, or read in bed for 15 minutes, 12 to 24 books is realistic. If you have small children or a heavy work season, 6 to 12 is a perfectly good goal. Audiobooks, short books and re-reads all count if you say they do. It is your list.

Choose a goal that has some slack

A useful trick: set your goal one step below what you think you can do, then treat anything extra as a bonus. Finishing a modest goal in October feels far better than limping toward a big one in December. You can also set a range, such as "12 to 18", so there is a win built in.

Three ways to track your reading

You don't need an app. Pick one of these and stick with it for the year.

  • The simple list. One line per book: title, author, date finished, and a star rating. It takes thirty seconds and gives you a clean record to look back on in December.
  • The bookshelf fill-in. Draw or print a bookshelf with one spine per book on your goal. Each time you finish a book, color in a spine. It's visual, a little addictive, and you can see at a glance how the year is going.
  • The reading log with notes. Add a few lines about what you liked, a favorite line in your own words, and where you read it. It's slower, but it turns a list into a memory you want to keep.

Build a tiny reading habit

Goals are easier when the habit is anchored to something you already do. A few ideas:

  • Keep a book where you sit for your morning tea or coffee.
  • Read one chapter before you pick up your phone at night.
  • Put your current book in your bag so waiting rooms become reading time.
  • Pair reading with a cozy cue: a lamp, a blanket, a favorite mug.

The no-guilt March check-in

Most reading goals wobble around March. Schedule a check-in now, on the first Sunday of each quarter, and ask three questions:

  • How many books have I finished, and how many did I expect to?
  • What got in the way: time, energy, or the wrong book?
  • Do I need to change the goal, the book, or the routine?

If you are behind, you have three honest options. Lower the number, switch to shorter books for a while, or drop a book that isn't working. Quitting a book you dislike is not failure. It is making room for one you will finish.

Your 2027 plan in five minutes

  • Choose a number based on minutes per day, not on what other people post.
  • Pick one tracking method: list, bookshelf or log.
  • Set a reading spot and a daily cue.
  • Put four check-in dates in your calendar.
  • Decide now that falling behind just means adjusting the plan.

A reading year is long, and a gentle goal you finish beats a grand one you abandon. Happy reading.
