How to Plan a Vegetable Garden for Beginners (Small Space, One Page, Plus a Winter-Sowing Timeline)

December is the quiet month when a garden is easiest to plan. Nothing is growing, the seed catalogs are out, and a few notes on paper now save a lot of guessing in spring. This guide is for beginners with a small space: one raised bed, a patch of yard, or a few pots on a balcony. The goal is a plan that fits on one page.

Step 1: Pick five crops you will actually eat

The most common beginner mistake is planting what looks good on the seed rack instead of what ends up on your plate. Write down the vegetables and herbs you already buy every week. Then choose five.

A friendly starter list for many home gardens:

  • Lettuce or salad greens (quick to grow, cut-and-come-again)
  • Tomatoes (one or two plants go a long way)
  • Basil (grows well next to tomatoes)
  • Carrots or radishes (good for filling gaps)
  • Bush beans or peas (easy to sow straight into the soil)

Swap in whatever your family eats. Five crops is plenty for a first year, and it keeps the plan readable.

Step 2: Check your sunlight

Most vegetable crops that produce fruit, such as tomatoes, peppers and beans, want about six to eight hours of direct sun. Leafy greens and herbs tolerate more shade. On a sunny day, check the spot in the morning, at noon and in the late afternoon, and jot down how many hours of sun it gets. Even a rough count helps you decide where each crop goes.

No sunny yard? A balcony or a few pots by a bright wall works well. Just make sure every pot has drainage holes.

Step 3: Sketch the space and place your crops

Draw a rectangle for your bed or a circle for each pot. Put tall plants, such as tomatoes on a cage or trellis, on the north side so they do not shade shorter crops. Put low crops and greens in front.

Always follow the spacing printed on your seed packet or plant tag. Spacing differs by variety, so treat the examples below as a starting idea, not a rule.

Example A: a 4x8 raised bed

  • North end: two tomato plants, each in a cage or on a stake
  • Beside them: three or four basil plants
  • Middle: a block of bush beans or peas on a small trellis
  • South half: two short rows of lettuce, with carrots or radishes between

Example B: a three-pot balcony garden

  • Pot 1 (large, deep): one tomato, with basil tucked in at the edge
  • Pot 2 (wide and shallow): lettuce or salad mix, resown every few weeks
  • Pot 3 (medium): a mix of herbs you cook with, such as parsley, chives and thyme

Keep your sketch simple. You can always change it once the seeds arrive.

Step 4: Know your last-frost date

Almost every date in a garden calendar counts back from your average last spring frost. Look it up for your area using a local extension office or a regional frost-date chart, and write it at the top of your page. Every date below is a rough guide, so adjust it to your own frost date.

Step 5: Follow a December-to-May timeline

This timeline suits many cold-winter climates. Warmer areas will start earlier, and some may be able to sow outdoors all winter.

  • December: Choose your five crops, sketch the bed, and take stock of the seed and supplies you already have.
  • January: Order seeds. Start winter sowing hardy seeds (see below). Clean and sort your pots and tools.
  • February: Keep winter-sown containers outdoors. Plan where your tomato and pepper seedlings will sit under a bright window or grow light.
  • March: Start tender crops such as tomatoes and peppers indoors, about six to eight weeks before your last frost, or as your seed packet says. Direct-sow very hardy crops outdoors once the soil can be worked.
  • April: Harden off seedlings by giving them short outdoor visits that gradually get longer. Sow peas, lettuce and radishes outdoors as the packets suggest.
  • May: After your last frost, plant out tomatoes and basil, and sow beans. Water steadily and mulch.

What is winter sowing?

Winter sowing is a low-cost way to start seeds outdoors in the cold months, using a mini greenhouse made from a clear container with drainage holes, such as a milk jug. You fill it with moist potting mix, sow seeds, tape the container shut, and leave it outside. The seeds sprout when the weather is right for them.

A simple way to try it:

  • Cut a clear jug around the middle, leaving a hinge at the handle.
  • Poke drainage holes in the bottom and a few vent holes in the top.
  • Add a few inches of moist potting mix and sow your seeds.
  • Tape the jug closed, label it with a permanent marker, and set it outdoors where it gets sun and some rain or snow.

Winter sowing works best for cold-hardy seeds, including many greens, along with hardy flowers and herbs. Tender crops like tomatoes, peppers and basil will not like freezing weather, so start those indoors later. Check the back of each seed packet to see whether a seed is hardy and when to sow it.
